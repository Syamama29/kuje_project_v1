Our History

Trace the remarkable journey of Kuje Area Council from its founding to its emergence as a vital administrative and agricultural hub in the Federal Capital Territory.

The Beginning

Origins of Kuje Area Council

Kuje Area Council was established as one of the six Area Councils in Nigeria's Federal Capital Territory (FCT), Abuja. The creation of Area Councils was a deliberate policy by the Federal Government to bring governance closer to the people at the grassroots level, ensuring that the dividends of democracy reach every community within the nation's capital.

The indigenous people of Kuje are predominantly of the Gbagyi (Gwari) ethnic group, known for their rich cultural heritage, agricultural expertise, and peaceful coexistence with other ethnic communities. Over the decades, Kuje has welcomed settlers from across Nigeria—Hausa, Fulani, Yoruba, Igbo, and many others—creating a vibrant, multicultural society.

Historical Journey

Key Milestones in Kuje's Development

A chronological overview of the significant events that have shaped Kuje Area Council into what it is today.

1
1976

Creation of the Federal Capital Territory

The Federal Capital Territory was created under the leadership of General Murtala Mohammed, with Abuja designated as the new capital of Nigeria. The territory was carved out from parts of Niger, Nasarawa (then Plateau), and Kogi (then Kwara) states. This marked the beginning of Kuje's transformation from a rural settlement to an important administrative area.

2
1980s

Establishment of Kuje as an Area Council

Kuje was formally constituted as one of the six Area Councils within the FCT Administration framework. The council was established to provide local governance, collect revenues, and deliver essential services to communities within its jurisdiction. Early council activities focused on basic infrastructure, market development, and agricultural support for the predominantly farming population.

3
1990s

Growth & Expansion of Council Secretariat

As Abuja developed rapidly, Kuje experienced significant population growth and urbanization. The Council Secretariat was expanded to accommodate growing administrative needs. Key departments including Works & Housing, Health Services, and Agriculture were strengthened to meet the demands of an expanding population.

4
2000s

Recognition as "Food Basket of the FCT"

Kuje gained prominence as the "Food Basket of the FCT" due to its vast arable land and high agricultural output. The council intensified support for farmers through fertilizer distribution programs, agricultural extension services, and the development of rural access roads to facilitate the transportation of farm produce to Abuja markets.

5
2010s

Infrastructure & Urban Development Era

Significant investments were made in road construction, rural electrification, water supply projects, and the expansion of primary healthcare facilities across the 10 wards. The council also launched youth empowerment programs, scholarship schemes, and skills acquisition initiatives to address unemployment and promote self-reliance.

6
2020s

Digital Transformation & Modern Governance

The council embraced digital transformation with the launch of its official website, e-services portal for tax payments, and online platforms for citizen engagement. Strategic partnerships were formed with development agencies to improve sanitation, healthcare delivery, and environmental sustainability across all communities.

7
2026

Present Day – A Model Area Council

Today, Kuje Area Council stands as a beacon of grassroots governance in the FCT. With over 300,000 residents, 10 vibrant political wards, and a diversified economy spanning agriculture, commerce, and services, Kuje continues to grow as a model of inclusive development, transparent administration, and community-driven progress.

Heritage & Identity

Rich Cultural Heritage of Kuje

Kuje Area Council is home to a rich tapestry of cultural traditions, primarily rooted in the Gbagyi (Gwari) heritage. The Gbagyi people are renowned for their agricultural prowess, intricate pottery, traditional wrestling festivals, and vibrant masquerade displays that celebrate the cycles of planting and harvest.

Over generations, Kuje has become a melting pot of cultures, with Hausa, Fulani, Yoruba, Igbo, and numerous other ethnic groups contributing to the area's diverse cultural landscape. This harmonious coexistence is reflected in the community's festivals, markets, and daily life.
